## Chapter 1. Introduction to Texas

### Part A — Summative items (not seen by students)

**1.A1** *(Outcome 8 · Analysis)* A researcher finds that Texans consistently favor low taxes, limited regulation, and deference to established business leaders, while showing little interest in using government to reduce inequality. Using Elazar's typology, this pattern is best described as:

- A. Moralistic
- B. A blend of traditionalistic and individualistic **← correct**
- C. Purely moralistic with individualistic elements
- D. Egalitarian

> *Rationale:* Elazar's traditionalistic culture emphasizes preserving the existing social order under established elites; individualistic culture treats government as a marketplace serving private and economic interests. Texas is generally classified as a blend of the two.

**1.A2** *(Outcome 8 · Application)* A demographer notes that Texas has had no racial or ethnic majority since 2004 and that one group became the largest in roughly 2022. That largest group is:

- A. Non-Hispanic white Texans
- B. Black Texans
- C. Hispanic Texans **← correct**
- D. Asian Texans

> *Rationale:* Texas lost its white majority in 2004 and by 2022 Hispanic Texans had become the largest single group, at roughly 40 percent, essentially even with non-Hispanic white Texans.

**1.A3** *(Outcome 1 · Comprehension)* Which sequence correctly orders these events in Texas history?

- A. Republic of Texas, Mexican independence, statehood, secession
- B. Mexican independence, Republic of Texas, statehood, secession **← correct**
- C. Statehood, Mexican independence, Republic of Texas, secession
- D. Secession, Republic of Texas, statehood, Mexican independence

> *Rationale:* Mexico won independence from Spain in 1821; Texas won independence from Mexico in 1836; Texas joined the United States in 1845; Texas seceded in 1861.

**1.A4** *(Outcome 8 · Analysis)* The 1901 Spindletop gusher is treated as a turning point in Texas history primarily because it:

- A. Ended Reconstruction in Texas
- B. Transformed a largely rural agricultural economy into an industrial one **← correct**
- C. Established the Texas Railroad Commission
- D. Caused Texas to secede from the Union

> *Rationale:* Spindletop launched the oil boom that industrialized the Texas economy, reshaping its population, politics, and public revenue.

**1.A5** *(Outcome 7 · Application)* A student argues that Juneteenth deserves its status as a holiday because it marks a legal turning point rather than a cultural celebration alone. The strongest support for that argument is that June 19, 1865 marks when:

- A. The Texas Constitution was ratified

> *Rationale:* Juneteenth commemorates the arrival of Union troops in Galveston enforcing the emancipation of enslaved Texans.

> *Answer:* Spain, France, Mexico, the Republic of Texas, the Confederate States of America, and the United States.

## Chapter 2. The Texas Constitution

### Part A — Summative items (not seen by students)

**2.A1** *(Outcome 1 · Analysis)* Texas's 1876 constitution created a weak governor, a part-time legislature, and elected judges. The best explanation for this cluster of choices is that the framers were reacting against:

- A. Federal overreach during the Mexican-American War
- B. The centralized Reconstruction government of Governor E. J. Davis **← correct**
- C. The failures of the Republic of Texas
- D. Excessive power held by county governments

> *Rationale:* The 1876 framers deliberately fragmented and weakened state government in reaction to the Davis administration's centralized Reconstruction-era power.

**2.A2** *(Outcome 1 · Application)* A legislator wants to amend the Texas Constitution. Which sequence is required?

- A. A two-thirds vote of each chamber, then approval by a majority of voters **← correct**
- B. A majority vote of each chamber, then the governor's signature
- C. A two-thirds vote of each chamber, then the governor's signature
- D. Approval by voters, then ratification by the Legislature

> *Rationale:* Texas constitutional amendments require a two-thirds vote of all members elected to each chamber and then approval by a majority of voters. The governor plays no formal role.

**2.A3** *(Outcome 3 · Analysis)* Article II of the Texas Constitution forbids a person attached to one department of government from exercising power belonging to another, yet the lieutenant governor presides over the Senate. This is best characterized as:

- A. A violation that courts have struck down
- B. An express exception within the constitutional design that blurs the separation in practice **← correct**
- C. Evidence that Texas has no separation of powers
- D. A federal requirement imposed on Texas

> *Rationale:* Article II permits exceptions expressly made in the constitution. The lieutenant governor, elected as an executive officer but presiding over the Senate, is a notable case where the branches overlap by design.

**2.A4** *(Outcome 3 · Comprehension)* Which pairing correctly matches a Texas branch with a check it holds over another branch?

- A. Judiciary: calling special sessions
- B. Legislature: overriding a veto by two-thirds of each chamber **← correct**
- C. Governor: impeaching state officials
- D. Judiciary: confirming gubernatorial appointments

> *Rationale:* The Legislature can override a gubernatorial veto by a two-thirds vote in each chamber. Special sessions are called by the governor, impeachment begins in the House, and confirmation is a Senate power.

**2.A5** *(Outcome 1 · Analysis)* Critics call the Texas Constitution excessively long and frequently amended. Supporters of the design would most likely respond that its length and amendment rate:

- A. Prove the document is poorly written
- B. Reflect deliberate limits on government that require voter approval to change **← correct**
- C. Show that Texas courts refuse to interpret it
- D. Result from federal requirements

> *Rationale:* Because the 1876 document restricts government in detail rather than granting broad authority, changes usually require amendment, which requires a popular vote. Defenders view this as keeping power with the people.

## Chapter 3. Federalism

### Part A — Summative items (not seen by students)

**3.A1** *(Outcome 2 · Application)* A state law conflicts with a valid federal statute enacted within Congress's constitutional powers. Under the supremacy clause, the result is that:

- A. The state law prevails because states are closer to the people
- B. The federal law prevails **← correct**
- C. A federal court must first ask the state to repeal its law
- D. The two laws operate simultaneously

> *Rationale:* The supremacy clause makes valid federal law supreme over conflicting state law, provided the federal law is within the powers the Constitution grants.

**3.A2** *(Outcome 2 · Analysis)* Texas charges higher tuition to out-of-state students at its public universities. This is permissible under the privileges and immunities clause because:

- A. The clause applies only to criminal matters
- B. States may treat residents and nonresidents differently in limited ways such as residency-based benefits **← correct**
- C. The clause was repealed by the Fourteenth Amendment
- D. Education is exclusively a federal responsibility

> *Rationale:* The clause bars discrimination against out-of-state citizens in fundamental matters, but states retain latitude for limited distinctions such as tuition and residency requirements.

**3.A3** *(Outcome 2 · Comprehension)* Which best describes the significance of McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)?

- A. It established that states may tax federal institutions
- B. It established implied powers under the necessary and proper clause and affirmed national supremacy **← correct**
- C. It limited the commerce clause to the buying and selling of goods
- D. It gave states the right to secede

> *Rationale:* McCulloch upheld the national bank under the necessary and proper clause, establishing implied powers, and held that a state may not tax a federal institution.

**3.A4** *(Outcome 2 · Analysis)* A policy analyst describes a federal program in which Washington provides money for a broad purpose and lets each state design its own program with few restrictions. This best describes:

- A. A categorical grant under cooperative federalism
- B. A block grant, associated with new federalism **← correct**
- C. An unfunded mandate
- D. Dual federalism

> *Rationale:* Block grants provide funds for a broad purpose with few strings, giving states flexibility. They are the signature tool of new federalism.

**3.A5** *(Outcome 3 · Analysis)* Which statement most accurately distinguishes federalism from separation of powers?

- A. Federalism divides power among branches; separation of powers divides it among levels
- B. Federalism divides power among levels of government; separation of powers divides it among branches at the same level **← correct**
- C. They are two terms for the same concept
- D. Federalism applies only to Texas; separation of powers applies only to the federal government

> *Rationale:* Federalism divides authority between national and state (and local) levels. Separation of powers divides authority among legislative, executive, and judicial branches within a level.

### Part C — Chapter review questions (short answer)

**3.C1** How does federalism differ from the separation of powers?

> *Answer:* The separation of powers divides authority among branches at the same level; federalism divides authority between levels — national and state.

**3.C2** Distinguish enumerated, reserved, and concurrent powers.

> *Answer:* Enumerated powers are granted to the national government; reserved powers are left to the states (Tenth Amendment); concurrent powers are exercised by both.

**3.C3** What did McCulloch v. Maryland establish?

> *Answer:* That Congress has implied powers under the necessary and proper clause (upholding the national bank) and that states cannot tax federal institutions — affirming national supremacy.

**3.C4** What was the significance of Gibbons v. Ogden?

> *Answer:* It defined interstate commerce broadly (including navigation) and affirmed that federal commerce power overrides conflicting state laws.

**3.C5** Contrast dual and cooperative federalism.

> *Answer:* Dual federalism (“layer cake”) kept national and state powers separate; cooperative federalism (“marble cake”) blends them, with jointly funded and administered programs.

**3.C6** What is a block grant, and which model favors it?

> *Answer:* Federal money for a broad purpose with few strings; new federalism favors block grants to give states flexibility.

**3.C7** How did the Civil War and its amendments change the federal balance?

> *Answer:* They ended the right of secession, established federal supremacy, and (through the Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendments) extended federal protections against the states.

## Chapter 4. The Texas Legislature

### Part A — Summative items (not seen by students)

**4.A1** *(Outcome 4 · Application)* A bill passes the Texas House and Senate in different forms late in the session. The next procedural step is most likely:

- A. The governor chooses which version to sign
- B. A conference committee works out the differences **← correct**
- C. The bill dies automatically
- D. The Texas Supreme Court resolves the conflict

> *Rationale:* When the chambers pass different versions, a conference committee of members from both chambers negotiates a single version for final approval.

**4.A2** *(Outcome 4 · Analysis)* An observer claims the lieutenant governor is more powerful than the governor of Texas. The strongest evidence for this claim is that the lieutenant governor:

- A. Can veto legislation
- B. Controls Senate committee assignments and the flow of legislation **← correct**
- C. Appoints all state agency heads
- D. Serves as chief justice of the Texas Supreme Court

> *Rationale:* As presiding officer of the Senate, the lieutenant governor controls committee assignments, referral, and the calendar, giving that office extensive influence over what becomes law.

**4.A3** *(Outcome 4 · Comprehension)* Texas legislators are paid $7,200 per year plus a per diem while in session. The most commonly cited consequence of this compensation structure is that it:

- A. Guarantees a legislature that mirrors the state's demographics
- B. Favors candidates with independent wealth, flexible professions, or outside income **← correct**
- C. Requires legislators to meet year-round
- D. Is set annually by the Legislative Budget Board

> *Rationale:* Low pay tends to restrict who can realistically serve, favoring those with independent means or flexible careers, a common critique of the citizen-legislature model.

**4.A4** *(Outcome 4 · Application)* The governor wants the Legislature to address a single urgent issue in August of an odd-numbered year, after the regular session has ended. The governor may:

- A. Extend the regular session by 30 days
- B. Call a special session limited to 30 days and set its agenda **← correct**
- C. Order the Legislature to convene for 140 days
- D. Pass the measure by executive order

> *Rationale:* Only the governor can call a special session; it may last up to 30 days, and the governor sets the subjects the Legislature may consider.

**4.A5** *(Outcome 6 · Analysis)* In redistricting, 'cracking' refers to:

- A. Concentrating a group's voters into as few districts as possible
- B. Dividing a group's voters among many districts so they cannot form a majority in any **← correct**
- C. Drawing districts of unequal population
- D. Redrawing districts more than once per decade

> *Rationale:* Cracking splits a group across districts to dilute its voting strength; packing concentrates the group into few districts to limit its influence elsewhere.

### Part C — Chapter review questions (short answer)

**16.C1** What is the difference between civil liberties and civil rights?

> *Answer:* Civil liberties are freedoms from government (limits on what the state may do to you); civil rights are guarantees of equal treatment by government.

**16.C2** How does the Texas Bill of Rights differ from the federal one?

> *Answer:* It comes first (Article I) rather than as later amendments, is longer and in places broader, includes an Equal Rights Amendment the nation never ratified, and can be read by Texas courts to protect more than the federal minimum.

**16.C3** How did the federal Bill of Rights come to apply to Texas?

> *Answer:* Through the Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause and selective incorporation, which applied most federal rights to the states one at a time.

**16.C4** What did Texas v. Johnson hold, and why does it matter?

**16.C5** Name the three landmark Texas civil rights cases of the 1940s-50s and what each did.

**16.C6** What are the three levels of scrutiny?

**16.C7** What is the current status of Texas's Ten Commandments classroom law?

> *Answer:* A district judge blocked it in 2025; the full Fifth Circuit upheld it 9–8 in April 2026; the challengers have asked the U.S. Supreme Court to hear the case.

### Part C — Chapter review questions (short answer)

**18.C1** Why must the Texas Legislature delegate authority to agencies?

> *Answer:* It meets only 140 days every two years and cannot write rules detailed enough to govern every situation, so it passes enabling legislation granting agencies authority to fill in details and carry out the law.

**18.C2** How are most Texas agencies governed, and why does it limit the governor?

> *Answer:* By multimember boards appointed to staggered terms. Because terms overlap, a governor inherits predecessors' appointees, needs years to reshape a board, and generally cannot remove appointees at will.

**18.C3** How does the Sunset process work?

> *Answer:* Each covered agency has a statutory expiration date and is automatically abolished unless the Legislature passes a bill to continue it. Sunset staff evaluate the agency, the commission holds public hearings and adopts recommendations, and the Legislature votes.

**18.C4** What has Sunset accomplished, and what are its limits?

> *Answer:* Since 1977, 95 agencies abolished (42 outright, 53 consolidated), about $1 billion in savings, roughly $16 returned per $1 spent. Critics note most reviewed agencies are continued, abolitions usually involve small entities, and the 12-year cycle is long.

**18.C5** What is administrative rulemaking, and how can the public participate?

> *Answer:* Agencies write rules carrying the force of law. Proposed rules are published in the Texas Register for public comment, and the agency must consider and respond to significant comments before the rule takes effect.

**18.C6** What is regulatory capture, and how does it happen?

> *Answer:* An agency comes to serve those it regulates rather than the public — usually not through corruption but through dependence on industry expertise, lopsided participation in proceedings, and the revolving door.

**18.C7** What rights do Texans have to monitor agencies?

> *Answer:* The Public Information Act allows anyone to request records without stating a reason, and the Open Meetings Act requires public notice and open meetings of governmental bodies.
